# Goal 5, milestone 5: can outages be predicted?

Generated by `scripts/research_report.py` from queries 30-31.

**Question:** using only what the public hourly data showed up to last night, can we tell which reactors will start
an outage in the next 7 days?
**Short answer: no.** Warning signs exist on average, but they are weak, and a model built on them scores close to a
coin flip on years it hasn't seen.

## A. Setup (query 30)

One row per reactor per day it was running at midnight: 35,518 reactor-days. Warning signs use only
hours before the day begins; the outcome uses only hours from that day on. Outcome: an outage of a day or longer,
not an announced refurbishment, starts within 7 days. A second, stricter outcome leaves out outages guessed
"likely planned" (query 28), because a planned outage is on the calendar and reactors are often turned down on
purpose before it.

Base rate: **3.37%** of reactor-days are followed by an outage within 7 days
(2.42% for the stricter outcome).

**Interpretation:**
- Several signs roughly double the odds: reduced power in the last week, output that wandered, a recent outage.
- **None is usable alone.** With the strongest sign present, about 94 days in 100 still pass without an outage, and
  no sign is present before more than about a quarter of outages.
- **Long runs don't raise risk.** A reactor running for over a year is barely more likely to go down than average, so
  risk doesn't build up with time since the last outage.
- **Part of the signal is the operator's own plan.** Leaving out "likely planned" outages shrinks most signs. The
  heaviest "partly running" group (over 3 days in the last week) is *less* likely than average to be followed by an
  unplanned-like outage.

## C. A model with all the signs together

**Method:** logistic regression with 11 inputs (the signs above, plant and season), standardised.
Trained on days before 2024-01-01 (22,963 reactor-days), tested on days from 2024-01-01
(12,460), with a 7-day gap so no outcome straddles the split.
**Scores:** AUC = the chance the model ranks a random day that was followed by an outage above a random day that
wasn't (0.5 = coin flip). Brier skill = how much better the predicted probabilities are than always predicting the
base rate (0% = no better). Intervals re-draw whole reactors.
**Result:**

**Interpretation:**
- **On unseen years the model is a coin flip:** AUC 0.515 (95% interval 0.472 to
  0.572) against 0.618 on the data it learned from. Brier skill is
  -0.3%: no better than quoting the base rate.
- **It's the same at every cut-off:** 0.59-0.70
  on training data, 0.50-0.57 on the next
  year, and fading in the latest years.
- **Eleven inputs do no better than one.** "Hours partly running in the last week" alone scores the same.
- **The risk ranking is not reliable:** the fifth of days the model called riskiest did not have the most outages.
- Almost every odds ratio's interval includes 1. With 18 reactors, the model cannot tell real effects from quirks
  of individual reactors.

**Conclusion:** hourly output and capability do not carry enough information to predict outages a week ahead. The
in-sample signs are real descriptions of the past (part of them the operator's own planned turn-downs), not a
forecasting tool. The people who can predict outages have what public data lacks: maintenance schedules, inspection
results, equipment condition.
**Limitations:** this rules out *these inputs and this kind of model*, not prediction in general. 18 reactors and
about 190 outages is a small sample. Other horizons (1 day, 30 days) and richer public sources (CNSC event reports)
were not tried.
