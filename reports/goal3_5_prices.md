# Goal 3.5: why do nuclear outages move the price so little?

Generated by `scripts/research_report.py` from query 32. Prices are the old market's hourly price (HOEP), May 2019
to April 2025, 52,608 hours. The new market's prices are not mixed in.

**The puzzle (from query 11):** when nuclear is 500+ MW below its monthly average, the wholesale price is only about
$4/MWh above its monthly average.

## A. The size, with honest error bars

**Method:** compare each hour with its own month's average (removes season, fuel prices, the long refurbishments),
then fit a line: price difference against nuclear capability difference. Controls are added one at a time.
Standard errors are clustered by week, because prices in neighbouring hours move together and treating
52,608 hours as independent would overstate certainty.
**Result:**

| model | $/MWh per 1,000 MW of nuclear lost | 95% low | 95% high |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same month only, treating every hour as independent (what a naive test would report) | 4.2 | 3.72 | 4.67 |
| Same month only, honest error bars (hours grouped by week) | 4.2 | 2.79 | 5.6 |
| + same hour of the day and weekday/weekend | 4.25 | 2.86 | 5.64 |
| + holding demand fixed | 4.47 | 3.34 | 5.6 |
| + holding demand and wind fixed | 5.24 | 4.24 | 6.25 |
| Price spikes capped at the 99th percentile (same controls as row 3) | 3.82 | 2.57 | 5.06 |
| Placebo: nuclear from 4 weeks in the future (should be near 0) | -0.2 | -1.99 | 1.58 |

| hours | price_rise_per_1000_mw_lost | correlation | pct_of_price_movement_explained | typical_price_swing | typical_nuclear_swing_mw |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 52608 | 4.2 | -0.07 | 0.57 | 29.1 | 522 |

**Interpretation:**
- **About $4.20 per MWh for every 1,000 MW of nuclear lost** (95% interval $2.79 to
  $5.60). Every version of the check gives $3.82 to $5.24.
- **The error bars matter.** Treating hours as independent gives an interval about a third as wide as the honest one.
- **The placebo finds nothing,** as it should: nuclear capability from four weeks in the future has a slope of
  $-0.20 (interval $-1.99 to $1.58).
- **The effect is real but small next to everything else:** nuclear explains 0.57% of
  the price's movement within a month. The price typically swings $29 around its monthly
  average for other reasons.

## B. Explanation: other sources fill the gap

For every 1,000 MW of nuclear output missing (line fits, each hour against its own month):

| hours | more_gas_mw | more_hydro_mw | more_wind_solar_mw | more_other_mw | more_net_imports_mw | lower_demand_mw | adds_up_to_mw |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 65040 | 395 | 78 | 89 | 3 | 368 | 68 | 1,000 |

**Supported, and the largest reason.** Gas covers about 395 MW and trade about
368 MW. Ontario is normally a net exporter, so the trade part is mostly selling less to
neighbours. The pieces add up to 1000 MW, as they must.

## C. Explanation: outages are timed for when they matter least

| 1 | 893 | 1,278 | 0.82 |
| 2 | 148 | 1,046 | -1.67 |
| 3 | -651 | -175 | -4.79 |
| 4 | -590 | -1,623 | -10.86 |
| 5 | -807 | -1,747 | -9.91 |
| 6 | 392 | -53 | -3.26 |
| 7 | 897 | 1,363 | 6.29 |
| 8 | 725 | 1,157 | 12.2 |

**Supported.** Nuclear capability is lowest in March-May and October-November, the months demand is lowest, and
highest in January, July and August, when demand peaks. Note this seasonal pattern is *removed* by the within-month
comparison in A, so it is an extra reason the yearly bill is affected little, on top of the $4.20.

## D. Explanation: it depends how high demand is

| hours_with | hours | avg_demand_mw | avg_gas_mw | avg_price | pct_hours_price_5_or_less | price_rise_per_1000_mw_lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. low demand | 17536 | 13,257 | 953 | 17.74 | 27.1 | 3.88 |
| 2. middling demand | 17536 | 15,459 | 1,829 | 27.88 | 10.9 | 4.15 |
| 3. high demand | 17536 | 17,532 | 2,856 | 40.74 | 3.5 | 5.03 |

**Supported, modestly.** The slope rises from low-demand to high-demand hours. In low-demand hours the price is at
$5 or less 27% of the time: with surplus power, losing some nuclear
barely moves the price.

## E. Explanation: bigger outages hurt disproportionately

| nuclear_vs_usual | hours | avg_nuclear_vs_usual_mw | price_vs_usual | price_rise_per_1000_mw_lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. 1,500+ MW below usual | 510 | -1,836 | 3.92 | 2.14 |
| 2. 1,000-1,500 below | 1288 | -1,202 | 7.42 | 6.17 |
| 3. 500-1,000 below | 5762 | -678 | 3.72 | 5.49 |
| 4. within 500 of usual | 38330 | 19 | -0.29 |  |
| 5. 500-1,000 above | 4973 | 683 | -2.22 | 3.25 |
| 6. 1,000+ MW above usual | 1745 | 1,309 | -6.11 | 4.67 |

**Not supported.** The price difference does not grow faster than the shortfall. The largest group
(1,500+ MW below usual) has only 510 hours, so it is uncertain.

## F. A sharper test: the day after an outage starts

**Method:** for each outage, average price in the 24 hours after it starts minus the 24 hours before, minus the same
difference exactly one week earlier (removes day-of-week patterns). Intervals by bootstrap.
**Result:**

| outages | n | price change, day after vs. day before | minus the same change a week earlier | 95% low | 95% high |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| All outages | 164 | 4.7 | 5.47 | 2.5 | 8.44 |
| Guessed 'likely unplanned' | 32 | 9.82 | 10.77 | 2.75 | 19.93 |
| Guessed 'unclear' | 85 | 3.73 | 5.93 | 2.07 | 9.96 |
| Guessed 'likely planned' | 47 | 2.99 | 1.02 | -2.96 | 4.97 |

**Interpretation:** outages guessed "likely unplanned" are followed by a rise of about $11/MWh
(interval $2.75 to $19.93, n = 32); those guessed "likely planned" by
$1.02 (interval includes zero). That fits a market that can prepare for a planned outage and not
for a surprise. It also supports the milestone 3 guesses from a direction they never used.

## Not tested

**The Global Adjustment.** Most of what Ontario pays for electricity is a charge outside the hourly price that pays
generators regardless of it. If the hourly price is a small part of the cost, a small price effect says little about
the total cost of an outage. Testing this needs IESO's Global Adjustment data, which is not in the database (the
build environment could not reach the IESO site to add it).

**Limitations:** all of A-E is association. Controls help, and the placebo and the day-after test make coincidence
less likely, but an unobserved driver of both outages and prices can't be ruled out. Demand is computed from
generators of 20 MW or more plus net imports. The planned/unplanned split in F uses guesses, not confirmed labels.
